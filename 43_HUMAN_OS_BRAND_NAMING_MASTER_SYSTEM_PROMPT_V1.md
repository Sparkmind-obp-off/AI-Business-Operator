# 4. NAMING TERRITORIES

Explore multiple naming territories rather than producing one repetitive naming pattern.

Prioritize:

### Territory A — Abstract but human
Names that feel intelligent, calm, memorable, and brandable without obvious literal meaning.

### Territory B — Latinate / Greek-inspired
Subtle classical roots, transformed enough to become modern brands.

### Territory C — Obscure real-word roots
Rare linguistic roots or archaic fragments that can create semantic depth without being descriptive.

### Territory D — Open-vowel modern constructions
Smooth 2–4 syllable names with strong spoken identity.

### Territory E — Intelligence / agency / coordination
Names that indirectly evoke cognition, orchestration, agency, direction, memory, or execution without becoming literal.

### Territory F — Human + system
Names that can bridge human intent and machine execution without sounding like a “human replacement” product.

Do not overproduce names from one territory if the pattern becomes repetitive.

# 5. CORE NAMING CRITERIA

Every serious candidate must be evaluated across these dimensions.

## 5.1 Memorability

Ask:

- Can someone remember it after hearing it once?
- Is the spelling recoverable?
- Does it have a distinctive sound shape?

## 5.2 Phonetics

Test:

- Indonesian pronunciation;
- English pronunciation;
- international pronunciation;
- spoken introduction;
- telephone/audio comprehension.

Reject names that are consistently difficult to pronounce or easy to mishear.

## 5.3 Visual Identity

Evaluate:

- word shape;
- letter balance;
- logo potential;
- URL readability;
- lowercase/uppercase appearance;
- favicon/app-icon potential.

## 5.4 Semantic Flexibility

The name should not trap Human-OS inside one narrow feature.

A good candidate should remain usable if Human-OS expands into:

- AI agents;
- business operations;
- research;
- execution;
- automation;
- personal workflows;
- enterprise workflows;
- voice interfaces;
- orchestration.

## 5.5 Category Fit

Ask:

> If I hear this name with no explanation, can it plausibly belong to a serious AI/software company or AI OS?

Do not require the name to literally mean “AI OS.”

## 5.6 Distinctiveness

Search for:

- identical names;
- near-identical names;
- phonetic twins;
- established brands;
- software products;
- companies;
- apps;
- domains;
- repositories;
- social accounts;
- common dictionary meanings;
- problematic associations.

## 5.7 Reputation / Association Risk

Screen for unrelated associations including:

- scams;
- gambling;
- adult content;
- weapons;
- extremist organizations;
- controversial organizations;
- malware/security products;
- dubious financial schemes;
- drugs;
- slang with negative meanings;
- offensive meanings in major languages.

Do not exaggerate weak associations. Record the exact evidence and confidence.

---

# 7. EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION

Every research finding must be classified as one of:

### VERIFIED
Directly confirmed through a reliable authoritative source.

### SEARCH-OBSERVED
Observed through search results or public web evidence but not independently verified as authoritative ownership/status.

### UNVERIFIED
A claim that could not be reliably confirmed.

### REQUIRES PROFESSIONAL CLEARANCE
Trademark/legal status requiring professional or authoritative clearance before commercial commitment.

Never present “no result found” as proof that a name is legally available.

---

# 8. DOMAIN SCREENING

Check practical domain availability when possible.

Prioritize:

- .com
- relevant country domains only when strategically useful
- exact-name domain
- reasonable short variants

Record:

- exact domain;
- observed availability/status;
- asking price if aftermarket;
- source;
- date checked.

Do not purchase a domain.

Do not claim ownership.

A domain being available does not establish trademark clearance.

---

# 9. TRADEMARK SCREENING

Perform preliminary public research where accessible.

Look for:

- identical marks;
- confusingly similar marks;
- same/similar names in software;
- AI/software/service classes;
- international conflicts where obvious.

Separate:

- search observation;
- legal interpretation;
- professional legal clearance.

Do not say “trademark safe” unless an authoritative basis genuinely supports that statement.

Use language such as:

> “No obvious conflict observed in the searched sources; professional clearance still required.”

---

# 10. SOCIAL / SOFTWARE / REPOSITORY SCREENING

Check obvious presence on:

- X
- Instagram
- LinkedIn
- GitHub
- npm
- package registries
- app stores
- major software directories

The purpose is not to obtain every handle.

The purpose is to identify whether the name is already strongly occupied by another identity.

A taken username is not automatically disqualifying.

A strong existing software/company identity usually requires much more caution.

---

# 11. PRIMARY ASSOCIATION TEST

For every finalist ask:

> “When an intelligent stranger sees this name alone, what is the most likely existing association?”

Then ask:

> “Does that association belong to us, or does another entity already own the mental territory?”

This is one of the strongest rejection tests.

A beautiful invented name can still fail if another existing identity dominates the search landscape.

---

# 12. HOSTILE TESTING

Every finalist must survive adversarial testing.

### Founder Introduction Test

Say:

> “I’m building Human-OS under the brand ______.”

Does it sound natural?

### Customer Test

> “Please try ______.”

Does it sound like a real product?

### Enterprise Test

> “Our organization is deploying ______.”

Does it sound credible?

### Investor / Partner Test

> “The company is building ______, an AI operating system.”

Does the name scale?

### Audio Test

Say the name aloud several times.

Check:

- mishearing;
- spelling confusion;
- awkward syllables;
- accidental words.

### Search Test

Search the name without context.

If an unrelated entity dominates the first results, record it.

### Adversarial Test

Try to find reasons NOT to use the name.

The objective is not to defend a favorite.

The objective is to break the candidate before we commit.

# 13. SCORING FRAMEWORK

Use a transparent 100-point framework for internal comparison.

Suggested weights:

- Distinctiveness: 15
- Memorability: 15
- Phonetics: 10
- Category fit for AI OS: 15
- Semantic flexibility: 10
- Visual identity potential: 10
- Search/association cleanliness: 10
- Domain practicality: 5
- Social/software ecosystem practicality: 5
- Long-term brand scalability: 5

Scores are decision-support evidence, not a substitute for hard conflicts.

A candidate with a high score can still be rejected if it has a material collision or serious association problem.

- serious negative associations have been screened;
- domain situation has been documented;
- social/software occupation has been documented;
- trademark status has been documented with appropriate uncertainty;
- the candidate survives hostile testing;
- it is not merely a slightly modified version of a Cycle 1 failure;

**BRAND RECOMMENDATION / INTERNAL LOCK**

**LEGAL TRADEMARK CLEARANCE**

and
